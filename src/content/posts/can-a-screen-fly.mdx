It started with one lazy sentence I typed into a chat window: *I am thinking of a flying screen that follows me so I work without sitting and I can work out.*

I pace when I think. I also sit in front of a monitor for ten hours a day, which is not great for a body. So the picture was simple. A display hovering beside me, at eye height, that drifts along when I walk to the window and turns when I turn. Work while standing, work while moving, maybe even work while doing something that looks a bit like exercise.

My worry was obvious too. A drone that carries a screen needs motors. Motors are mass. More mass needs more thrust, which needs bigger motors and a bigger battery, which is more mass. Does that loop ever stop, or does it run away?

That question turned into a co-design engine, a long [paper](/papers/flying-screen/), an independent review that found real bugs, and a design called DESK-1/W. Short version: the loop closes, for half an hour, and energy turns out to be the easy part.

## The loop that might not close

Write the coupling down and it looks like this:

mass sets the thrust you need to hover, thrust sets the power (momentum theory), power over the session sets the energy, energy divided by the battery's specific energy sets the battery mass, and the battery is part of the mass.

If you push every term through, the whole sizing problem collapses into one line:

$$
\alpha m - \beta m^{3/2} = M_0
$$

Here $m$ is the gross mass, $M_0$ is everything you carry that does not scale with the aircraft (the screen, the computer, the gimbal), $\alpha$ is what is left of each kilogram after structure and motors take their share, and $\beta$ collects the flight time, the battery, the rotor size and the efficiencies.

The left side is not monotonic. It rises, peaks, and falls. So there is a maximum payload, $M_0^{\max}$, and the paper proves a clean trichotomy: below it there are exactly two solutions (a light one, which is your aircraft, and a heavy unstable one), at it they merge, and above it there is no solution at all. Not a heavy drone. No drone. That is a saddle-node bifurcation, and it is the reason this needed a paper instead of a spreadsheet. If you iterate the naive way (guess a mass, size the battery, add it up, repeat), you converge to the light root when it exists and diverge when it does not.

The scary part is how hard endurance bites. The wall goes as one over the flight time squared:

$$
M_0^{\max} = \frac{8\,\alpha^3 \rho A\, e_b^2\, \mathrm{FM}^2 \eta^2}{27\, g^3\, t_f^2}
$$

Four times the flight time costs you sixteen times the payload. (The test suite asserts that exponent comes out at exactly $-2$.)

When you open the tool on its defaults (a 13 inch panel, four 40 cm rotors, 250 Wh/kg cells, a 10 percent reserve and a one hour session) it says **no solution**. The wall is 0.60 kg of effective fixed load and the design is asking for 0.77 kg. At eight hours the wall falls to about 9 grams. My original fear was correct, for long sessions.

## The fear was the wrong fear

Cut the session to half an hour and the algebra relaxes completely. At 20 minutes the critical payload is 6.37 kg against a requirement of 0.57 kg. Half an hour of flight costs about a quarter of a kilogram of cells. Energy is not what stops this product.

Four human requirements stop it instead:

- **Noise.** A quiet office is about 45 dBA, normal speech about 60. You need to stand next to this thing for thirty minutes and think.
- **Downwash.** Loose paper starts moving at roughly 5 m/s of wake.
- **Size.** It has to live beside a desk.
- **Clearance.** It is spinning blades near your face.

All four pull the same way: big, slow, lightly loaded rotors. And the one that binds hardest is noise.

Broadband rotor noise goes with the sixth power of tip speed. Halving the tip speed is worth 18 dB. That one exponent drove the whole design to four 492 mm two-blade paddle rotors turning at 1036 rpm, a tip speed of 26.7 m/s, against roughly 68 m/s for a commercial camera drone. To get enough thrust at that speed the blades are wide (82 mm chord), which also means the propeller and its low-KV motor are custom parts. Nothing you can order.

## The 14 dB mistake

My favourite failure in the project, because it was so quiet (sorry):

The first version of the acoustic model used an anchor constant I had more or less guessed. Then I tried to calibrate it against real aircraft. EU regulation 2019/945 makes drone manufacturers declare a sound *power* level. I had been treating that number as a sound *pressure* at one metre. Those are different quantities. The conversion over a reflecting floor is about 8 dB, and once I ran four commercial aircraft back through the model properly, the anchor moved to 82.9 dB from the 68 I had assumed. The four aircraft, which span 3.6 times in mass, agree with each other to within 1.5 dB, which is what makes me trust the corrected value.

Total error before the fix: 14 dB. That is wider than the entire design space. Every noise number the engine had produced before that day was wrong by more than any design choice I could make.

Then there is the room. The level usually quoted at one metre is a free field number, as if the machine hovered over an endless flat plain. Indoors you also get a reverberant field that does not fall off with distance at all, so the room adds to what reaches your ear. Carpet and curtains help the rest of the room more than they help you, because at your position the direct sound still dominates. The current design is 53.8 dBA free field at one metre and **56.6 dBA at your ear**. A loud desk fan. You notice it, and you can work beside it.

And the number still hides something. The blade passage frequency is about 35 Hz, which A-weighting discounts heavily. Low frequency hum is felt more than heard, and four rotors at slightly different speeds beat against each other. None of that is in 56.6 dBA, so a real prototype could be unpleasant at a level the model calls fine.

## Your face is not at the centre of mass

The second surprise was geometric. I had specified a standoff: the screen stays 0.7 m from my eyes. Fine. But the standoff is measured to the *display*, and the rotors reach most of a metre back from it, toward me. A safety check at the vehicle's centre was wrong by the whole reach of the machine.

The safety analysis pushed the standoff to 1.2 m, which keeps the blade tips about 0.59 m from your face. And then the third surprise arrived.

## A resolution is not a workspace

At 1.2 m, a 15.6 inch 1920 by 1080 panel is *sharper* than a desktop monitor: 117 pixels per degree. Sharpness is fine. The problem is letters. A 16 px line of body text subtends 5.8 arcminutes at that distance, and the ergonomics standard (ANSI/HFES 100) asks for at least 16. To meet it you scale the interface by about 278 percent, which leaves a logical desktop of 692 by 389 pixels.

The resolution is there. The usable workspace is a tablet's. Move closer and the text gets readable but the blades get to within 15 cm of your eyes. You can have a safe flying screen or a full desktop on a flying screen, not both, at this panel size. A bigger panel would fix it, and the acoustics will not allow a bigger panel. Readability is an angle, not a pixel count.

And pixel density does not pick the panel either. Areal density does: a desktop monitor panel is two to three times heavier per square metre than a laptop panel, which is the difference between a 1.7 kg aircraft and one over 4 kg that breaks every limit.

## The hardest move is turning around

Once the machine exists, the flight simulation takes over: full 6 degree of freedom dynamics, a geometric attitude controller, and a separate gimbal loop that keeps the screen upright while the airframe tilts.

Walking is easy. Pacing at 1.4 m/s costs a fraction of a percent more power than hovering. Jogging in a loop, about 4 percent. The killer is a person turning 180 degrees in about a second with the screen expected to stay in front of their face. Tracked rigidly, that asks for more than 10 m/s² of swing: the airframe hits 64 degrees of tilt, the motors saturate a third of the time, and the display goes 26 degrees off upright while a 1.7 kg machine whips past your head.

The fix was to stop chasing the face. A reference governor caps the screen at 2.5 m/s and 2.5 m/s², lets it fall behind and catch up, and keeps a keep-out sphere that moves with your head. With it on, the same turn costs about 5.5 percent over hover and the rotors never come closer than 0.61 m to the eye. The product requirement became "how much lag is acceptable", not "how fast can it follow".

There is also an irony in yaw. A quiet rotor is a slow rotor, and a slow rotor makes very little reaction torque, which is where a multirotor gets its yaw authority: about 56 and 60 rad/s² in roll and pitch, only 4.5 in yaw. So yaw goes on the gimbal, and the airframe keeps whatever heading it likes.

## DESK-1/W

Running all of that together, with the battery loop closed around the flight simulator, gives:

- 1.724 kg quadrotor, 1.26 m folding span
- four 492 mm two-blade paddle rotors at 1036 rpm
- a 15.6 inch laptop panel plus a Raspberry Pi thin client and a separate flight controller
- 106 W in hover, 30 minutes to a 12 percent reserve, about 28 minutes if you turn around all session
- 53.8 dBA free field at 1 m, 56.6 dBA at the ear
- standing 1.2 m back, blades about 0.59 m from your face

And the version I would actually want: on a 37 g, 48 V tether it is lighter (1.62 kg), quieter, and never stops. 48 V stays under the safety extra-low voltage line. The tether is not a fallback, it is the primary mode, with the battery there for when you walk away from the desk.

## The review that found what the tests did not

When the engine and the paper were done, every test passed. Then an independent review went through the derivations and the code with a reproduction script, and found real problems. Two examples:

- The component solver searched for a feasible mass on a grid that stepped by 1.35 times. Near the fold, the window of feasible masses can be narrower than one grid step. The review found a case at a 4280 second mission with two valid roots, 4.568 kg and 4.617 kg, that the engine reported as "no mass closes". The paper had also called that failure "infeasibility".
- The control authority divided the balanced rotor differential by the number of rotors, a factor of four on every axis, and that error had flowed into the gain rule and every layout comparison.

Both are fixed, along with three more, each with a regression test. Passing tests check that the code agrees with itself and with the closed forms I wrote. They do not establish the claims. A second reader with a script does. (That review gets its own post.)

## What it does not answer

No hardware was built. Every number here is the output of a model, and the paper lists where those models are weak. The biggest open item is recirculation: the rotors move a normal room's entire volume of air through their disks about every 21 seconds, so within a minute the machine is flying in its own wake. A quadrotor with this controller cannot survive a motor failure. Human tracking is assumed perfect; a 100 ms estimator delay at walking speed moves the keep-out sphere by 14 cm, more than the whole margin.

So the most valuable next step is not a better model. It is one 492 mm paddle rotor on a thrust stand, measuring thrust, power, rpm and the noise spectrum. That single measurement would settle most of the uncertainty in the design.

If I started again, I would calibrate against real hardware on day one. A unit mistake was worth more than every design decision combined, and I only found it because I went looking for real aircraft to compare against.

The full derivation and the design study are in the paper: [The Flying Screen](/papers/flying-screen/).
